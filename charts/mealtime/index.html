Mealtime: notes for grown-ups

Regular meal and snack times, eaten together at the table, are the routine. The steps around them make it predictable.

Young toddler, 12–23 months

  • They learn the routine by doing it with you over and over. You do most of the work; they "help".
  • Order matters far more than the clock. Same steps, same order, every time.
  • Words for time ("in five minutes") mean nothing yet. Use what comes next: "after the bath, pajamas".
  • Big feelings at transitions are normal. Saying "no" is a sign of growing independence, not defiance.

Two-year-old, 2 to 3 years

  • "Me do it!" Give them a real job in each step, even if it takes longer, and finish the hard part yourself.
  • Two choices beat a question: "red cup or blue cup?", not "do you want to brush teeth?".
  • Expect stalling at bedtime and short meltdowns at transitions. Stay calm and keep the steps the same.
  • Many children show signs of being ready for the potty between 18 and 30 months. There is no fixed age.

Three-year-old, 3 to 4 years

  • Can do several steps alone with reminders, and loves checking off a picture chart.
  • Starts to understand "first, then": "first teeth, then story".
  • Puts on loose clothes and shoes, uses a fork. Buttons and zips are still hard.
  • Testing limits is part of the job. A routine lets the chart be the boss instead of you.

Four- and five-year-old, 4 to 5 years

  • Can run most of a familiar routine alone, with you checking in.
  • Ready for small real responsibilities: setting a place at the table, packing their bag.
  • Can help plan the routine and pick the pictures, which makes them far more willing to follow it.
  • Can understand a simple clock or timer ("when the big hand is on the 6").

Six- and seven-year-old, 6 to 7 years

  • Can run a familiar routine alone, with a checklist to keep it on track. You check in rather than direct.
  • School uses up a lot of their self-control. Tired, hungry or tearful after school is common, especially early in the year.
  • Ready for real jobs: packing their own bag, helping make their lunch, feeding a pet.
  • Starts reading a clock, but still can't judge how long things take. Mornings need a "leave in 10 minutes" warning.

Step by step

🧺 Toys away
Puts a few toys in a bin with you. Make it a game.Tidies with you; needs one clear direction at a time ("blocks in the box").Tidies one area alone with a reminder. Picture labels on bins help.Tidies a room with a checklist and a clean-up song.Tidies their room with a checklist; usually needs a reminder to start. Fewer toys out means a manageable clean-up. Use the same clean-up song every time.
🧼 Wash hands
You wash their hands; name each step.Washes with help reaching the tap.Washes alone with a step stool.Washes alone.Washes alone. Same place, same order, before every meal: it becomes the cue that food is coming.
🍽️ Help set the table
Not usually part of this routine yet at this age. Leave it out, or do it for them.Can carry their own unbreakable cup or spoon to the table.Can put out napkins or spoons for everyone.Can set a place with a picture placemat to copy.Can set the table for everyone. Real jobs build cooperation. Use unbreakable dishes so mistakes don't matter.
🪑 Sit at the table
In a high chair or booster, strapped in. Meals are short: 10–15 minutes.Sits for 10–15 minutes. Getting up means the meal is over.Sits for about 15 minutes with family conversation.Sits for 15–20 minutes and can join conversation.Sits through a 20-minute family meal and joins the talk. No screens at the table. Eat together when you can: children copy what they see you eat.
🍎 Eat
Appetite swings a lot day to day. Offer one food they like alongside new ones.Picky eating peaks around now. A new food can take 10–15 tries before it's accepted.Can say when they're full. Avoid "one more bite" deals.Can help choose between two healthy options and try a "learning bite".Can serve themselves. Still decides how much; keep offering new foods without pressure. You decide what is served, when and where; they decide whether to eat and how much. Keep meal and snack times regular.

Thank them for the specific job ("you carried your bowl all the way") rather than a general "good job".
